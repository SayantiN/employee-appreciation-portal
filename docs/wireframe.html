Design system — quiet base, muted accents

The base is restrained so the product reads as a serious HR system. Colour is then spent deliberately and semantically: every hue means one thing, everywhere. All six accents are desaturated and share a similar depth, so none of them shouts over the content or over each other — and none is used as a large wash.

The rule that makes it work One hue, one meaning, product-wide. Sage always means “live or good”. Bronze always means “time is running out”. Clay always means “you cannot”. Plum always means “an award”. A user who learns that on the Dashboard can read the Winners Report without being taught it again. The moment sage is used decoratively, the system stops carrying information and becomes noise.
Contrast — one value per hue, no exceptions list Every accent here clears 4.5:1 on white as small text: blue 5.8, plum 5.5, sage 4.8, bronze 5.1, clay 5.4, teal 5.0. The earlier brighter set did not, and needed a second darker value for every hue just to label anything. Dropping the saturation removed that whole category of mistake — there is now one hex per role, used for fills, borders, icons and text alike.
2.2 — Where colour is not allowed at all

Restraint, deliberately

  • Not on the voting form. The form is a writing surface. Colour there competes with the person’s own thinking. The only colour is the rating fill and validation — look at 6.4 below and count the accents.
  • Not behind body text. No tinted card backgrounds under paragraphs.
  • Not as the only signal. Every status carries a word as well as a hue — colour-blind users and greyscale printouts must still work A-8.
  • Not in large fields. Outside the login panel, the dashboard banner and the winner banner, an accent never occupies more than roughly 5% of the viewport — and even those three are now mid-tone rather than vivid.
2.3 — Type & spacing
1112141620243244

Type & spacing rules

  • One geometric-humanist sans for display and UI. Tabular numerals — vote counts and ratings sit in columns and must align.
  • Body 16px minimum on mobile. Below that, iOS zooms the viewport on focus and it feels like a bug RR-4.
  • 4px base grid. Card padding 16 / 20 / 24. Page gutters 16 / 24 / 32 for mobile / tablet / desktop.
  • Radius 8px cards, 6px controls, 999px pills. One elevation, for overlays only — static cards get a border, not a shadow.

Design logic

  • The mobile four are chosen by frequency, not by org chart. Dashboard and Vote are the monthly task; Feedback is the reward that brings people back; Winners is the social pull. Reports and Admin are deliberate destinations — burying them behind More costs a power user one tap and saves every other user four wrong ones.

  • The icon rail expands over content rather than pushing it, so a laptop user’s reading position never jumps.

Design logic

  • Every column sorts; every column filters, with the control matched to the data type — text contains, enum multi-select with counts, number range, date range with presets, person search, boolean DG-1 DG-2.
  • AND across columns, OR within one. Stated in the UI, not left to be discovered.
  • Filter chips are non-negotiable. The commonest support ticket in any grid product is “rows are missing” from a filter the user forgot. Chips with individual × make the cause visible DG-6.
  • Mobile keeps everything. Cards replace the table, but the two sheets above expose every capability desktop has. Dropping filters on small screens is the standard shortcut, and this spec closes it DG-15 AC-13.
  • Server-side above 200 rows. Fetching everything and filtering in the browser breaks at scale and ships data the user may not be entitled to DG-9.
  • Sort and filter column names are allow-listed. A grid is a query builder pointed at your database — treat its input as hostile NF-9.
  • Restricted columns are excluded server-side, never hidden with CSS. A hidden column whose data is in the payload is a leak, not a permission DG-17.

Design logic

  • One call to action, not a menu of them. The banner states the single most urgent thing with its deadline and one button. After voting it changes to “Your vote is in — editable until 30 Sep, 6:00 PM.” A dashboard that offers six equal options gets none of them done.
  • The banner is the only large blue field here; the four tiles stay white with coloured values and chips. Colouring all four would flatten the hierarchy the banner just established.
  • Participation is a count, never a standing. While a cycle is open, no per-nominee number may reach an employee — not in the UI, and not in any API response their role can call R-6.3.1 BR-5. This is the rule most likely to be broken by a well-meaning “show top 3” addition.
  • Sage and bronze sit side by side in the first tile — the cycle is live, and time is running out. Two facts, two hues, no sentence needed.
  • The hatched feedback block is deliberate. Rather than hiding withheld content, the Dashboard shows that something exists and says when it will appear. Silence reads as a bug; an explained absence reads as a rule.
  • Getting Started is the only teal element on this page and disappears permanently once the playlist is done. Persistent onboarding becomes furniture people stop seeing.
  • Winner cards are violet-bordered, not violet-filled. Full plum is reserved for the moment of announcement on the Results and Showcase pages.
  • Mobile: tiles go to a 2×2 compact grid, the chart drops to 3 months with a table fallback RR-14.

Design logic

  • The form is the product, so it is the least colourful screen here. Count the accents: a sage and a bronze chip in the header, a blue focus ring, a clay validation state, a blue rating fill. Nothing else. A person writing an honest appraisal of a colleague should not be visually hurried.
  • Numbered steps 1–6. Six required inputs is a lot; numbering turns an intimidating wall into a countable task and makes “what’s left” answerable at a glance.
  • Helper text sits above each field, never in the placeholder. Placeholders vanish the moment you type — exactly when you need the prompt — and they break the label association screen readers need A-3.
  • Live summary rail, not a progress bar. It names which field is incomplete and why — “Comparison: Too short” — so the user never hunts. A percentage tells you nothing actionable.
  • The self-vote exclusion is shown, not silent. Someone who looks for their own name and cannot find it will assume a bug; one hatched line removes the doubt. Enforced three times regardless — absent from the list, rejected by the server, forbidden by a database constraint R-6.4.2 IR-1.
  • The anonymity disclosure is on the form itself, at the moment of decision, including the admin caveat. Burying it in Help would make the promise misleading R-4.1.
  • The rating is a slider with −/+ steppers and a large numeral. Sliders are unusable precisely on touch; the steppers give 44px targets without abandoning the at-a-glance scale RR-3.
  • The countdown is decoration. The server clock decides whether a submission is accepted, and if the window shuts mid-session the typed text is preserved and the user is told plainly R-6.4.1 R-6.4.9.
  • Draft is not a vote. Autosaves every 20s, stays private, never counts, and is discarded as non-participation if never submitted R-6.4.6.

Design logic

The rule that makes anonymity real Feedback appears only once the cycle has closed and the nominee has at least 3 votes in it. A single anonymous vote on a four-person team is not anonymous — the nominee can deduce the author from the fact that it exists. Without this rule the anonymity promise is decoration. R-6.7.2

Design logic

  • The suggestion is the only coloured element on each card — bronze bar, bronze ground. Praise is pleasant and gets read; the suggestion is the part that makes someone better and the part people skim past. Structure fixes that; asking nicely does not.
  • “A colleague” with a generic avatar — never an initial, never a department. On a small team a department label identifies a person as surely as a name R-6.7.1.
  • And it is not merely a UI rule. The API response behind this page carries no voter identifier in any form NF-10 AC-4.
  • Summary stats lead with volume and average, not rank. This page is about a person’s own growth, not their standing against colleagues — the one place in the product where comparison is deliberately absent.
  • The one win gets a plum card in the rail, tying this screen to the award language used everywhere else.
  • Report is quiet but present on every card — visible enough to use, subdued enough not to suggest abuse is expected R-6.7.4.
  • Mobile: single column, long text clamped to four lines with “Read more”, filters in a bottom sheet.

Design logic

  • The same cycle renders two completely different screens by role. Showing them side by side is the point: the hidden-results rule is a product behaviour, not a permission checkbox, and it must be visible in design review.
  • The hidden state explains itself. “Results are hidden” alone invites workaround requests; naming the reason pre-empts them.
  • Plum at full strength appears here for the first time. Winner cards elsewhere are violet-bordered; the announcement banner is violet-filled. That escalation is the palette doing its job.
  • The pre-close admin view is watermarked in solid ink. Anyone who screenshots it should be unable to pass it off as a result.
  • The tie-break ladder is published, not internal. Ties will happen in a 120-person company, and a rule announced afterwards always looks invented R-6.8.2.
  • Frozen at close. The tally is snapshotted the moment the window shuts; later corrections cannot silently rewrite a published result R-6.8.4 BR-9.
  • An admin override never rewrites the leaderboard. The computed order stands, and the override is recorded as a decision R-6.17.1.
  • A department below target turns bronze in the stats — the one place on this page where colour prompts action rather than reporting a fact.

Design logic

  • The onboarding playlist is one teal banner with a progress ring, not a checklist. A new joiner should see one button, not twelve.
  • The anonymity video is the only one marked Required, and it is marked in clay — the same hue the product uses for “you cannot”. The model has a subtlety, nominees cannot see voters but admins can, and relying on people to read a Help page is not disclosure R-4.1.

  • The script is the source of truth and is version-controlled with the video. When a feature changes you edit the script and regenerate the audio. That is the entire economic argument for a synthetic voice; a pipeline that loses it has bought nothing over hiring a narrator R-6.14.1.
  • Videos carry the app version they were recorded against and go bronze when the covered screen changes — visible to viewers, queued for the admin R-6.14.7.
  • Watch data never leaves onboarding. It drives resume-playback and the progress ring, and is barred from appraisal or any ranking report. Left unfenced, “who completed their training” becomes a performance metric in somebody’s review R-6.14.11 BR-16.
  • Completion registers at 90%, not 100% — nobody sits through the outro.
